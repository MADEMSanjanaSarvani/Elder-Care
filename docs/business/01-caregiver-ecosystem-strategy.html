Caregiver Ecosystem Strategy

How Project Setu sources, verifies, categorizes, pays, protects, and scales the caregiver side of the marketplace. This document does not re-decide anything already locked in the product PRD — it builds the operational depth underneath those decisions. Where a recommendation here would contradict something already built (payment model, verification vendor, pilot city, SOS design), that tension is called out explicitly rather than silently overridden.

Scope: Caregiver-side of the marketplace only — family/elder-side strategy is covered in docs/prd/ Geography: India, MVP in Visakhapatnam
Payments: merchant-of-record Vetting: clinical / non-clinical split SOS: 108-first mandatory BGV vendor: IDfy Pilot city: Visakhapatnam Consent: elder-granted, per category
Foundation

What this document inherits, and doesn't re-argue

Six decisions were locked before a line of this platform was built, and the schema, RLS, and Edge Functions already live in this repo enforce them. Re-deriving any of them here — even under headings like "Payment Model" or "City Launch Strategy" that invite a fresh comparison — would produce a document that contradicts the product. So: merchant-of-record payments, the clinical/non-clinical vetting split, 108-first SOS, IDfy as BGV vendor, Visakhapatnam as the pilot, and elder-granted per-category consent are treated as fixed inputs throughout. What follows is the operational detail underneath each of them — commission structure, onboarding SLAs, incident response, city-expansion criteria — the layer a technical PRD doesn't cover and a leadership team actually has to run the business on.

01 — Caregiver Acquisition Strategy

A hybrid model, split along the same clinical/non-clinical line already locked

The vetting split isn't just a verification-workflow decision — it implies a different sourcing strategy for each track, because the two labor markets behave completely differently.

TrackSourcing modelWhy
Clinical (nurses, physiotherapists)Partner with local home-nursing agencies and, where possible, hospitals — not individual onboarding as the primary channelAgencies already carry professional indemnity insurance and workman's comp, already maintain vetted pools facing a genuine national nursing shortage, and can push several credentialed staff through onboarding in the time one individual application takes. Liability for a solo clinical worker without institutional backing is a real, avoidable exposure at MVP scale.
Non-clinical (companion, hospital attendant, household support)Individual gig-style onboarding, platform-recruitedThis is where volume and cost-efficiency come from, and it's a labor market that already runs on individual gig platforms (Urban Company and similar). The trust-tier system is what makes individual sourcing safe here — it shouldn't be diluted by leaning on an agency's own, unauditable screening standard for this track.

MVP recommendation

One anchor clinical partnership (a home-nursing agency or hospital) to seed credentialed supply fast, paired with direct-to-consumer recruiting (social media, local community boards, referral incentives) for the non-clinical track. Don't attempt individual clinical onboarding at MVP — the volume doesn't justify the liability review overhead per caregiver yet.

City-to-city expansion

Replicate the anchor-partnership playbook per new city: one clinical partner signed before or immediately at launch, non-clinical recruiting opened in parallel. Resist opening a city on non-clinical supply alone — a platform with hospital companions but no verified nursing option undersells the core "elder care" promise, not just a companionship-visit app.

02 — Caregiver Onboarding Workflow

Two tracks, one converging into a trust tier

This maps directly onto the already-implemented caregivers table and computeTrustTier() logic — bgv_status, police_verification_status, professional_council_reg_no, insurance_on_file aren't abstractions here, they're the actual columns the onboarding flow populates.

Sequential steps

  1. Phone verification (OTP) — the cheapest possible spam gate, first thing, before any document collection effort is spent.
  2. Profile + category selection — which caregiver_type/sub_role the applicant is applying under; this determines which of the following steps are required.
  3. Government ID upload (Aadhaar/PAN) — stored as a caregiver_documents row, doc_type = 'gov_id'.
  4. Selfie / liveness verification — a real gap worth naming: the current schema's doc_type values (gov_id, police_verification, council_certificate, insurance) don't yet include a selfie-liveness check against the ID photo. This is a standard, low-cost fraud control (IDfy and most Indian KYC vendors offer it as part of the same integration) and belongs early in the funnel, before police verification is even initiated — no product reason to run a multi-week police process against an unverified identity.
  5. Address verification — in practice, largely subsumed into the police verification step below rather than a separate check.
  6. Background check via IDfy (the locked vendor) — criminal-record and available employment-history checks. This is the faster, largely automated track (bgv_status, driven by verification-idfy-webhook).
  7. Police verification — a genuinely separate, slower track by design (see § Legal & Compliance for why), typically conducted through the local police station covering the applicant's registered address, checking for a criminal record and a basic neighborhood character reference. No automated feed exists for this in most jurisdictions, which is exactly why police_verification_status is a manually-updated field in the schema, not a webhook-driven one like bgv_status.
  8. Professional certification (clinical track only) — nursing or physiotherapy council registration number, validated against the relevant state/national council registry.
  9. Insurance on file (clinical track only) — professional indemnity, before clinical_verified is reachable.
  10. Reference/background checks — prior employer references, weighted more heavily for the clinical track given the higher-trust, more independent nature of that work.
  11. Platform orientation and safety training — tracked as an onboarding checklist (informational, not currently gating trust tier — see the Caregiver Management System architecture for why that's a deliberate MVP boundary, not an oversight).
  12. Manual admin review and approval — a verification_agent-scoped reviewer confirms the full picture before advancing trust tier; nothing about this step is automatic.

Trust tier as the funnel's output, not a separate score

The already-implemented ladder — probationary (BGV cleared) → standard (+ police verified) → clinical_verified (+ council registration, insurance, clinical track only) — is the onboarding funnel's result, not a parallel scoring system layered on top. A caregiver's tier at any moment is a direct, deterministic function of which steps above are complete; there's no separate "trust score" to reconcile against it.

StepTypical SLAAutomated?
Phone + profileSame dayYes
ID + selfie livenessSame dayYes (vendor-dependent)
IDfy background check2-5 business daysMostly — webhook-driven
Police verification15-30 days, sometimes longerNo — manual, jurisdiction-dependent
Clinical credentials + insurance3-7 business daysNo — manual registry check
Final admin review1-2 business days after all inputs completeNo — human judgment call

Police verification is the pipeline's real bottleneck

At 15-30+ days, this single step dominates total onboarding time and is the least controllable. Two implications worth planning around: (1) a caregiver can and should reach probationary tier well before police verification completes, so supply isn't needlessly gated behind the slowest step for lower-risk, lower-trust-tier bookings; (2) city launch timelines need real buffer for this step specifically — don't plan a launch date assuming police verification turnaround matches the faster IDfy track.

Mostly already in the schema — one requested category doesn't map cleanly

The existing caregiver_sub_role enum already anticipates most of what's being asked for here. Presented as a mapping rather than a fresh category design, since re-inventing categories that already exist in the live schema would create drift between this document and the product.

"Emergency Support Caregiver" is a certification, not a job category

Recommend against adding this as a new caregiver_type/sub_role. Emergency-response readiness (basic life support / first-aid certification) is more naturally a cross-cutting credential any existing category can hold — a companion or a nurse can both be certified, and gating an entire new job category around it would fragment supply for no real benefit. This connects directly to the nearest-available-responder suggestion already architected in Enhanced Emergency Services (PRD Part 10): that feature should weight certified caregivers of any category more highly, not search a separate emergency-only pool.

04 — Payment Model

Merchant-of-record is locked. Here's the commission, cancellation, and refund detail underneath it

The founder decision was merchant-of-record: Setu is the transacting party, families pay Setu via Razorpay, Setu pays caregivers separately via RazorpayX. Comparing this against a pure marketplace or lead-gen model isn't this document's job — that comparison already happened. What's underneath it and genuinely undecided is commission structure, cancellation policy, and refund handling.

Commission structure — already correctly reflected in the seeded data

TrackCommissionWhy lower for clinical
Clinical (nursing, physiotherapy)15%Clinical labor is scarcer and has higher operating costs (insurance, certification maintenance) — a platform has to be more generous to attract and retain this supply, especially while competing against agencies and direct hire.
Non-clinical (companionship, hospital attendant, medicine pickup, grocery)20%A larger, less specialized labor pool with lower barriers to entry; standard marketplace commission economics apply more cleanly here.

Worth flagging that this isn't a new recommendation — it's a confirmation that supabase/seed.sql's existing per-service commission_pct values already implement exactly this split (15% for home_nursing/physiotherapy, 20% for everything else).

Payment model by phase

MVP
Pure marketplace commission

Pay-per-visit only, exactly what's implemented today. No subscription or retainer product yet.

Phase 2
+ Care Plans (subscriptions)

A monthly-fee bundle of recurring visits, layered on top of — not replacing — per-visit commission economics for anything beyond the plan's allocation. Already architected in docs/prd/09-prd-part9-business-layer.html.

Long-term
+ Managed care retainer

A premium concierge/care-coordinator tier (also already architected) carries its own retainer fee on top of underlying visit costs, for families wanting less self-service.

Cancellation policy

Family cancels >4h aheadFull refund. No cost to the caregiver's schedule that can't be absorbed.
Family cancels <4h aheadPartial charge (recommend 50%) — the caregiver's slot is often no longer reasonably re-fillable on short notice, and this compensates their blocked time.
Caregiver cancels / no-showFamily gets a full refund plus priority rebooking; the caregiver takes a strike against their standing — repeated occurrences should trigger an automatic trust-tier review (see § Operational Model).

Refunds and tax

Refunds process against the original Razorpay payment using payments.provider_payment_ref — the schema already separates the order reference from the payment reference specifically because a refund needs the latter, not the former (a real bug caught and fixed earlier in this project). On tax: seed.sql's tax_profile jsonb already carries an 18% GST placeholder on platform commission with an explicit "confirm with a CA before launch" note — restated here rather than silently assumed correct, since neither this document nor the engineering team is positioned to finalize an actual tax determination.

05 — Trust and Safety Architecture

Most of this is already built. The gaps are complaint handling and caregiver-initiated safety

What's already in place

  • Multi-level verification — the onboarding ladder in § 02.
  • Trust tier — vetting status (probationary/standard/clinical_verified), deliberately separate from...
  • User ratings — service-quality rating, architected in docs/prd/08-prd-part8-caregiver-and-platform-ux.html as a genuinely distinct signal from trust tier. Worth restating the same distinction here in business terms: a family should never read "clinical_verified" as "5-star caregiver" — one is a safety floor, the other is a satisfaction signal, and conflating them in product copy or ops decisions would corrupt both.
  • GPS tracking — already scoped, by design, to active-booking windows only. Not always-on background tracking of caregivers between jobs — a real privacy line worth defending even under pressure to "just track everyone always" for safety's sake; the active-booking scope already gives family/ops the safety-relevant signal without the privacy cost of continuous surveillance of gig workers' personal time.
  • OTP-based visit verification, 108-first SOS escalation, write-path audit logs — all live.

Real gaps worth naming

No caregiver-initiated safety alert exists yet

The SOS flow is elder/family-initiated only — it checks that the caller is the elder or a linked family member. A caregiver who feels unsafe at a booking (a hostile household member, an unsafe environment) currently has no equivalent in-app path to raise an alert. This is a genuine, currently-unaddressed operational safety gap for the workforce side of the marketplace, not just a nice-to-have — flagged here for product/ops leadership to prioritize, not solved in this document.

Complaint management needs a defined path, separate from ratings

A 1-2 star rating captures dissatisfaction after the fact; it isn't the right tool for an active concern (a pattern of lateness, inappropriate conduct, a safety-adjacent complaint that isn't an emergency). Recommend a dedicated "report a concern" flow, distinct from the star-rating system, routed to ops with a defined SLA — for example, 24-48 hour acknowledgment, 5-7 day resolution target — and escalation criteria for anything safety-adjacent to bypass the queue entirely.

Elder financial exploitation — a risk specific to this business, worth naming explicitly

Elder financial abuse by caregivers is a well-documented risk pattern in home-care industries generally, not a Setu-specific hypothetical. Recommend: platform terms explicitly prohibiting a caregiver from being named as a beneficiary, witness, or agent on any financial or legal instrument of an elder they serve; and pattern monitoring for warning signs (a booking pattern that suddenly concentrates on a single caregiver, unusual gift-giving language appearing in visit notes) as an ops review trigger, not an automated block — false positives here would be damaging to a caregiver's livelihood, so this should surface for human review, not autonomous action.

06 — Legal and Compliance

India-specific requirements underneath the already-built DPDP consent model

Background verification in India

Police verification for domestic/in-home workers typically runs through the local police station covering the worker's registered address — an inquiry process (sometimes handled through the same administrative channel as residential tenant verification) checking for a criminal record and basic neighborhood character reference, taking 15-30+ days depending on jurisdiction and season. This is why the schema keeps bgv_status (IDfy, largely automated) and police_verification_status (manual, no webhook feed) as two separate tracks rather than one — they genuinely are two different processes with two different reliability and speed profiles, and the platform's founders were right to model them separately.

Worker classification — a live regulatory risk, not a settled question

Independent contractor classification needs specific legal review under India's gig-worker provisions

Most home-care platforms in India classify caregivers as independent contractors/platform partners rather than employees, avoiding PF/ESI employer obligations. India's Code on Social Security, 2020 introduces gig- and platform-worker welfare provisions that are being notified and enforced unevenly state by state — and elder care specifically (a physically-present, schedule-directed, platform-matched service) sits closer to an employment-like relationship than, say, ride-hailing, which increases scrutiny risk. Recommend dedicated legal counsel review of this classification specifically under the current Code on Social Security provisions before scaling caregiver headcount significantly — this is not a question engineering or product can resolve on their own.

Liability, and a real cost of the merchant-of-record decision

Because Setu is merchant-of-record — the transacting party, not merely a connector — its liability exposure for incidents during service delivery is somewhat higher than a pure lead-generation marketplace would carry. This is a known, accepted trade-off of that already-locked decision (in exchange for cleaner payment UX and stronger platform trust with families), not a new problem — but it should inform insurance decisions: general liability and professional indemnity coverage sized for a merchant-of-record risk profile, confirmed with counsel and an insurer, not assumed to be the same as a lighter-touch marketplace would need.

Payment compliance

As a merchant using Razorpay (an RBI-authorized Payment Aggregator) rather than acting as its own PA, Setu's compliance obligations under the RBI's Payment Aggregator Directions largely flow through Razorpay's own authorization and merchant onboarding review — worth explicit confirmation with Razorpay's onboarding/compliance team rather than assumed, since this determination sits outside engineering's visibility.

Data protection — the caregiver side of DPDP, not just the elder side

The consent model and DPDP Act 2023 compliance already built (per-category elder consent, me-data-export, me-erasure-request) covers the elder/family data-principal relationship. Caregivers are also DPDP data principals for their own verification documents (government ID, police verification records, professional credentials) — a related but distinct compliance surface. Recommend confirming retention limits and consent language for caregiver-side documents receive the same rigor as the elder-side model already has, rather than being treated as "just internal HR-style records" outside DPDP's scope.

07 — Operational Model

The process layer around what bookings-match already automates

Assignment and SLA

Matching itself is automated (trust tier + region + service type, extended with a preferred-caregiver tiebreak per PRD Part 6). What's undecided operationally: target match-time SLAs — recommend under 15 minutes for standard bookings, near-immediate for anything SOS-adjacent — and what happens on a match failure. Today a "no eligible caregiver" condition returns an error to the family; operationally this should also trigger an ops alert for manual sourcing, not just a dead end for the customer.

Backup caregivers

Not currently modeled at the schema level (a booking has exactly one caregiver_id). Recommend an operational practice — not a new architecture decision here — of maintaining a per-region, per-service-type backup pool ops can manually tap when a primary caregiver cancels or no-shows close to visit time, with a target backup-dispatch SLA (for example, under 30 minutes for same-day urgent bookings).

No-shows and cancellations

Caregiver no-show: immediate strike, automatic backup dispatch attempt, service credit to the affected family. Recommend an automatic trust-tier review trigger after a defined threshold (for example, two no-shows within 30 days) rather than leaving pattern detection to manual ops vigilance alone.

Emergencies during a visit

The 108-first SOS flow already covers the elder/family side. The caregiver-initiated gap is named in § 05 and repeated here because it's an operational readiness question, not just a product one: if a caregiver needs help during a visit today, there is no in-app path for it.

Customer support tiers

Tier 1In-app chat/FAQ for booking and account questions
Tier 2Phone support for active-visit issues
Tier 3Ops escalation for disputes and safety incidents — the existing admin dashboard's Bookings & Disputes page is this tier's working tool

08 — City Launch Strategy

Why Visakhapatnam fits the pattern, and what to look for next

Visakhapatnam is already locked as the pilot; this section explains the underlying criteria so the next city choice isn't made from scratch.

What makes a good launch city

  • A significant population of adult children who've relocated — for work, often out-of-state or abroad — leaving elderly parents behind. This is the core customer insight the entire product is built around, and it's the single most important screening criterion.
  • Reasonable hospital and nursing-agency density, for the anchor clinical partnership described in § 01.
  • Adequate smartphone and digital-payment penetration among the family segment — critically, this is about the paying family member, not the elder, who may never touch the app directly.
  • Moderate cost of living — keeps caregiver take-home pay and family-facing pricing both viable without one squeezing the other.

Sizing an MVP launch

Suggested starting caregiver pool

30-50 vetted non-clinical caregivers and 10-15 clinical (nursing/physiotherapy) for the pilot area — enough supply density to keep match times fast without diluting early caregiver earnings and utilization, which is exactly the kind of thing that determines whether early supply sticks around long enough for the marketplace to reach real liquidity.

Minimum service catalog: the six already seeded (hospital companion, home nursing, physiotherapy, medicine pickup, grocery assistance, companionship visit) are correctly right-sized for an MVP launch — broad enough to cover the core use cases, narrow enough to keep the caregiver pool's category-mix requirements manageable.

Operational footprint: a small on-ground team is sufficient at this scale — one city ops lead plus one to two verification/support staff — precisely because trust and safety are largely software-enforced (RLS, trust tiers, the consent model) rather than requiring a large manual review apparatus. That balance shifts as volume grows — see § 09.

09 — Scaling Strategy

The region_id seam already solves the infrastructure problem. This is the human/process side

Multi-city and multi-country scaling is already an architectural non-event — every table that would otherwise hardcode a city or country carries a region_id instead, per the six-phase roadmap in the product PRD. What scaling actually strains first is the caregiver ecosystem's human processes, not the software.

Caregiver-side scaling

Acquisition should shift from platform-driven sourcing toward organic and referral-driven growth once the trust-tier and rating systems make caregiver earning potential visible — successful caregivers become the platform's best recruiters. Agency partnerships should also scale disproportionately faster than individual onboarding at volume, since one agency conversation can onboard twenty clinical caregivers where twenty individual applications take twenty times the review effort.

User-side scaling

Managed Elder Care Services (the care-coordinator concierge tier) becomes a more meaningful differentiator at scale, and Care Plans/Subscriptions improve retention and revenue predictability versus pure pay-per-visit — both already architected, not new proposals.

Ops-side scaling — where the real strain shows up first

Processes that are manually tractable at MVP scale (police-verification tracking, backup dispatch, complaint triage) need either better tooling or partial automation as volume grows by an order of magnitude. Care Analytics Dashboard (already architected) becomes essential here — not a nice-to-have reporting layer, but the actual instrument for spotting operational bottlenecks (a region's match-time SLA quietly degrading, a spike in a specific complaint category) before they become customer-visible failures.

Prioritized by what actually threatens a trust-dependent business

TrustA single publicized safety incident is disproportionately damaging for a trust-dependent brandAn incident-response and crisis-communication playbook prepared in advance, not improvised; marketing language must never oversell "verified" as "guaranteed safe" — that framing increases both reputational and legal exposure if something does go wrong

The trust risk is the one worth weighting above the others

Every other risk category has a conventional operational mitigation. Trust, once broken publicly in an elder-care business specifically, is unusually hard to rebuild — the customer relationship is built on a family trusting a stranger in their parent's home. This is the argument for prioritizing safety and verification rigor over growth speed at every stage, not a platitude: it's the actual asymmetry in this business's risk profile.
