The AI-Powered Elder Care Platform

Working codename "Project Setu" (Hindi/Sanskrit for "bridge") — a placeholder for internal use only, not a brand decision. Part 1 covers strategy and requirements; Part 2 (system & technical architecture) and Part 3 (execution, testing, deployment, scaling) follow once the open decisions below are resolved.

Status: Awaiting founder review Market: India (pilot city TBD) Stack: Flutter · Supabase/PostgreSQL · Razorpay · GCM/FCM · OpenAI · n8n · PostHog
01 — Executive Summary

A trust layer, not another booking app

Millions of Indian families now live one city — or one continent — away from an aging parent. The services that parent needs already exist in fragments: a nurse from one app, a doctor's appointment from another, groceries from a third, an ambulance number nobody has saved. What doesn't exist is a single place where a distant adult child can see, arrange, and trust all of it — and where the parent isn't reduced to a dependent, but stays the primary person in the loop.

Project Setu is that place: a marketplace of verified caregivers and service providers, coordinated through an AI assistant that handles the busywork of care (reminders, scheduling, visit summaries, translation) without ever making a clinical judgment call. The wedge is not any single service — it's the trust infrastructure underneath all of them: identity verification, live accountability during a visit, an emergency escalation path, and a consent model where the elder, not just the paying child, has a say in what's shared.

The plan is to prove this in one city first. Every workflow — caregiver vetting, dispatch, emergency response, payments — has to work at neighborhood scale, with real caregivers and real families, before it's worth the engineering cost of scaling it. National and international expansion is a Part 3 concern; Part 1 is about getting the first 100 households and 50 caregivers right.

02 — Market Research

The demand curve is not speculative

India's elderly population (60+) is growing faster than the population as a whole. It stood at roughly 104 million in 2011, is projected to reach 193.4 million by 2031 (Government of India, 2020 Population Projections Report), and the UN projects the 60+ share will exceed 20% by 2050 — roughly double today's share. In parallel, urban migration for work and the steady rise of NRI households means a growing fraction of that elderly population lives without an adult child in the same city, let alone the same house. This is the structural tailwind: demand for distance-spanning elder care isn't a bet on changing behavior, it's a bet on a demographic curve that is already committed.

The category has investor validation — Emoha (Series B), Samarth Elder Care, Yodda, Khyaal, and Age Care Labs (reported $9M Series B1) have all raised institutional capital, and press coverage routinely frames this as India's "silver economy." That's a double-edged signal: real demand, but also a market where several well-funded players already have multi-year head starts on caregiver networks and brand trust in specific cities.

Where the gap actually is

The competitive set below is mostly single-lane: home healthcare only, or emergency response only, or community/content only. None of the major players combine (a) on-demand booking across clinical and non-clinical services, (b) an AI layer that does real coordination work rather than a chatbot FAQ, and (c) a consent model designed around the elder as a person rather than as a dependent the paying child manages. That combination — not any single service line — is the differentiated position worth building toward, and it should be validated with real user interviews in the pilot city before being treated as fact.

04 — Critical Risks & Decisions Needed

Get these wrong and the rest of the PRD is moot

This is the section a founding CTO owes you before touching a database schema. Several of the features requested — marketplace payments, police verification, live location tracking, AI visit summaries — carry regulatory and liability weight that changes the technical design underneath them. Each item below needs a decision before Part 2 (system architecture) can be written correctly; building the schema first and retrofitting these later will be materially more expensive.

Marketplace commission vs. RBI's 2025 Payment Aggregator Directions

RBI's Payment Aggregators Directions, 2025 (effective mid-September 2025) require non-bank PAs to hold merchant funds in escrow with a scheduled bank, and explicitly bar a PA from running "marketplace business" — aggregating funds for merchants with whom it has no direct contractual relationship. A gig caregiver is not a registered "merchant" in any conventional sense. Before Razorpay integration is designed, we need to know: does Setu operate as merchant-of-record and pay caregivers as contracted vendors (a payroll/vendor-payment ledger), or does it use a compliant marketplace settlement product (e.g. Razorpay Route) with every caregiver onboarded as a KYC'd sub-merchant? These produce different database schemas, different tax/TDS handling, and different onboarding friction.

Blocks: DB schema, API designDecision needed: settlement model, with payments counsel

Who consents to the elder's data — the elder, or the paying child?

Under the DPDP Act 2023 / DPDP Rules 2025, the elder is the data principal for their own health and location data — consent must be explicit and informed, and blanket or implied consent is invalid. Unlike a minor, an elderly parent with full mental capacity cannot lawfully have their data consented-for by an adult child by default. A "family dashboard" that silently gives the paying child full visibility into a competent adult's health notes and live location is both a compliance problem and a real elder-autonomy/trust problem — the exact failure mode this platform claims to solve, not create. This needs to be a first-class product decision: what does the elder actively grant, per data category, and can they revoke it.

Blocks: data model, consent UX, family dashboard scopeDecision needed: consent model — elder-granted vs. child-managed, and how capacity/incapacity is handled

"Verified caregiver" hides two very different risk tiers

Nurses and physiotherapists are licensed professionals under statutes like the Indian Nursing Council Act and state physiotherapy councils, and in-home clinical procedures carry malpractice exposure. Companions, cooks, and household-service providers are a household-gig-labor risk profile — closer to Urban Company than to a hospital. Treating both as one "verification" pipeline under-vets the clinical tier and over-burdens the non-clinical tier. They need separate credential checks (professional-council registry lookups vs. police/background checks), separate insurance requirements, and likely separate legal engagement terms.

Blocks: verification workflow, insurance strategyDecision needed: split clinical vs. non-clinical vetting tracks; confirm platform-level liability insurance

Police verification is not an instant API

State-administered police verification can take weeks, which conflicts with wanting caregivers active for a city launch. A workable pattern is layered: an instant private background-check (e.g. a KYC/background vendor like IDfy, Karza, or AuthBridge) to unlock a probationary, supervised tier immediately, with full police verification required before a caregiver is eligible for unsupervised or overnight in-home work. Note also that criminal-record results are themselves sensitive data requiring strict access control and retention limits — this shouldn't be a plaintext column on the caregiver profile.

Blocks: caregiver onboarding funnel, trust-tier designDecision needed: background-check vendor; define trust tiers and what unlocks each

Emergency SOS must not imply an ambulance dispatch guarantee

The platform is not a licensed EMS provider. If the SOS button reads as "help is coming" without making clear that calling 108 (India's emergency number) is the primary action, a missed or delayed real emergency becomes a life-safety incident with direct legal exposure to the platform. The UX and Terms of Service need to be explicit that platform escalation (notifying family, nearby caregivers, on-call staff) runs in parallel with — never instead of — directing the user to call emergency services first.

Blocks: SOS UX, ToS languageDecision needed: sign off on "call 108 first" as the mandatory primary SOS action

AI boundaries need to be architecture, not just a policy sentence

"The AI must never diagnose or replace licensed professionals" is a requirement everyone will agree with and no LLM will reliably self-enforce by default. Summarizing "medical instructions" is one prompt-drift away from restating a dosage or interpreting a symptom. This needs to be designed as a constrained AI architecture in Part 2 — scoped system prompts, an output filter/classifier for medical-advice-shaped text, mandatory disclaimers on any health-adjacent output, and a hard rule that anything touching medication or symptoms routes to a human (family member or the actual clinician), not the AI, for judgment calls.

Blocks: AI architecture (Part 2)No decision needed yet — flagged so it's designed in from the start, not bolted on

Regulatory runway is real, but the design work isn't optional

DPDP enforcement is phased: the Data Protection Board is active now, consent-manager registration opens November 2026, and full enforcement with penalties up to ₹250 crore starts May 13, 2027. That's genuine time to build correctly — but explicit consent flows, a Data Protection Officer, breach reporting within 72 hours, and DPIAs for high-risk processing (which this platform's health + location data almost certainly qualifies as) are far cheaper to design in now than to retrofit onto an entrenched data model in 2027.

No decision needed yet — informs Non-Functional Requirements and Part 2 security architecture

06 — User Stories (representative sample)

Enough to shape scope, not the full backlog

  • As Ananya, I want to book a hospital companion for my father's appointment next Tuesday and get a plain-language summary afterward, so I don't have to interrogate him for details he'll downplay.
  • As Ananya, I want to be notified immediately if an SOS is triggered, with my father's location and the nearest verified responder's ETA, so I can act within minutes, not hours.
  • As Raghunath, I want to approve exactly what my daughter can see about me — my medication list, yes; my live location all day, no — and change my mind later.
  • As Raghunath, I want to interact with the app by voice in Telugu, since typing in English is a barrier I don't want to fight every day.
  • As Meena, I want to see the full job details (address, duration, what's expected) before I accept, and get paid within a guaranteed window after an OTP-verified visit completion.
  • As Nurse Priya, I want the app to show me exactly what was prescribed and by whom, without asking me to interpret or extend it.
  • As a Platform Admin, I want a single dashboard of every caregiver's verification stage (background check, police verification, credential check) and an immutable log of every access to an elder's health data.

08 — Non-Functional Requirements

The requirements that don't show up in a feature demo

  • SOS-path availability is a different SLA from the rest of the app — target as close to 100% as the architecture allows, with degraded-mode fallback (plain SMS/call) if the data connection or backend is unavailable.
  • Consent & privacy by design — every field touching health, location, or identity data is modeled with a consent scope and retention policy from day one, not added later; DPIA-ready by construction.
  • Accessibility — elder-facing screens support large text, high contrast, and voice interaction; this is a core requirement, not a nice-to-have, given the primary care-recipient persona.
  • Offline resilience — caregiver app functions (job details, OTP entry, visit logging) degrade gracefully in low-connectivity areas rather than blocking a visit.
  • Data residency — health and identity data for Indian users stored within India-region infrastructure, anticipating stricter localization norms as DPDP Rules mature.
  • Auditability — every access to an elder's health/location data is logged and attributable, queryable by the elder and by trust & safety.
  • Localization QA — all 10 launch languages tested for both correctness and dignity of tone (elder-facing copy reviewed by native speakers, not machine-translated only).
